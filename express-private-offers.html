Express Private Offers

Using AI technology to fast-track purchasing and pre-program AWS private offer creation.

Client

AWS Marketplace (in-house)

My Role

Senior UX Designer

Duration

6 months part-time (Mar–Aug 2025)

Status

Launched Nov 2025 at AWS Re:Invent

context.

AWS Marketplace is a curated digital catalog where customers find, buy, deploy, and manage third-party software, data, and services. There are currently two ways to buy products, each with meaningful tradeoffs.

1. Public Offer

The standard, non-negotiated agreement buyers accept when buying from a product listing. A buyer picks from a pre-existing menu of units, terms, pricing, and contract lengths.

Advantage: Almost instant.
Disadvantage: Fixed options only. Pricing is estimated and often inaccurate.

2. Private Offer

A custom quote for a product listing. A buyer negotiates with the seller for a tailored version with specific terms, pricing, or contract length.

Advantage: Exact offer tailored to the buyer's needs.
Disadvantage: Can take weeks of back-and-forth negotiation.

What about use cases where public pricing is too barebones but a private offer is too custom? What about sellers that want to serve smaller customers efficiently?

Express Private Offers: a third, in-between offer method that combines the best of both worlds — custom offers delivered within 12 hours via an AI-powered guided wizard.

challenges.

This was a 6-month effort during which I worked concurrently on this project plus three others. I completed 90% of the design before handing off for the November 2025 Re:Invent launch.

Can we add more AI?

A core reason this project was greenlit was an AWS-wide initiative to showcase AI in core functionality. I looked for every opportunity to fit AI into the buyer and seller journeys — whether as a more AI-forward overall form factor (a freeform agent concept) or in individual steps.

…but only as much AI as we can fit before November

Despite the company-wide initiative, real constraints held us back: limitations of current AWS AI implementation, a dependency on the parallel Agent Mode team, and engineering resource shortages ahead of the Re:Invent deadline.

research.

Before I started in March 2025, a preliminary design existed as a simple form for buyers. My first instinct was to test a chatbot form factor against that — not because it was obviously better, but because it needed to be tested.

5 variations × 2 form factors

I made 5 wireframe variations differing across multiple factors, each with both a chatbot and a form version. I randomized which version users saw first, then asked for their preference at the end.

Build 5 tests on UserTesting.com

I created 1 master script document and copy-pasted ~45 prompts per test into 5 separate tests on UserTesting.com. I recruited 5 enterprise AWS users per variation (excluding participants from previous variations) — 25 users total.

AI-assisted synthesis

I downloaded raw transcripts as an Excel file, annotated them, and fed the annotated data into AWS Cedric (now Amazon Q) to summarize. Key findings: users preferred the form wizard because they could see how many steps remained; 100% of users said they found the feature useful and would use it.

Major decisions after testing

Since users wanted more structure than a chatbot, I transformed the form factor into a wizard with structured, trackable steps. I proposed adding AI agent presence as a helper in each step, rather than as the main form factor.

design.

I spent approximately 1 month and 5 iterations per experience designing the seller and buyer experiences. After testing a straight form and a full chatbot, I landed on a multi-step wizard for both.

Underlying technology being designed at the same time

As I began designing, I became aware that AWS Marketplace's Agent Mode — the underlying technology this feature was built on — was being designed and developed in parallel and would launch the same day. I tried and failed to fit the seller experience into a true agent chatbot as a result of this real-time dependency.

How do buyers know the difference between purchase methods?

How do we educate buyers on the differences without completely replacing private offers? I had to design clear signposting at every entry point so buyers understood what they were opting into.

Unit descriptions lack so much information

How do we define units well enough that buyers can operate semi-autonomously? How do we convince sellers to input more details? This is a classic chicken-and-egg problem between seller input quality and buyer confidence.

My final designs (August 2025) — these are my latest updated designs at approximately 90% completion. I onboarded and handed over my files to another designer, who wrapped the design phase in time for the November 2025 launch. Below I explain the significance of certain pages and compare my designs against what was shipped.

Seller part 1 — About Express Private Offer & Enter Units

After sellers select their desired dimensions in step 1, they add more details in step 2. Since these buyers have no representative support, they need to know exactly what they're buying. With AI agent mode, sellers can use AI support to craft more detailed descriptions. Differences from live version: verbiage became more general, and the requirement for a detailed additional description was relaxed to a suggestion.

Seller part 2 — Configure Discount Strategy

Step 4 contains the most important settings: restricting buyer eligibility and offering special discounts based on dimensions, total contract value, and buyer-entered descriptors. A seller can select up to 2 discount strategies and the page auto-populates subsequent settings. Differences: no AI agent help for sellers (I had proposed this but it was removed); additional structure for qualifier selection was added by engineering.

Buyer part 1 — About Express Private Offer & Enter Units

A buyer starts by learning what an Express Private Offer is, why it differs from a custom private offer, and entering how many units they want to purchase. Differences: steps 1 and 2 were merged; comparison information was reduced; total price during unit entry was removed (I disagreed — research showed users felt more motivated with a semi-accurate total price); and the AI question feature was cut.

Buyer part 2 — Contract needs, Questionnaire, and Check eligibility

In steps 2 and 3, buyers enter their contract needs and information about themselves. They then preview their input and get the offer. Differences: the contract start date/duration section was reshuffled; a new EULA section was added (unclear why — there's no action available there); the eligibility check step was removed, so buyers don't know whether they qualify until after completing all steps and signing in.

result.

Express Private Offers launched in November 2025 at AWS Re:Invent 2025. As of April 2026, users are primarily using the quotes it generates to gather competitive pricing information between AWS and other vendors — a behavior that tells us far more than what they said on any business call.

Beyond this launch

Even without my final touches, this feature launched successfully. Improvements I'd advocate for in future versions: usability testing with sellers; more AI touchpoints for both buyer and seller; earlier entry point for seller configuration; detecting and requesting sign-in earlier than step 5; reorganizing CTAs on the product details page; and restoring the eligibility check step so buyers know whether they qualify before completing all steps.

learnings.
  • Stagger parallel launches. Building an underlying technology at the same time as an implementation of that technology creates real-time dependency that's genuinely hard to manage well.
  • Users are more creative than we expect. The unexpected competitive-benchmarking use case surfaced something research wouldn't have — behavior beats stated intent.
  • Incomplete AI investments don't pay off. Adding AI works when it's baked into the core value proposition, not bolted on at the edges under time pressure.
