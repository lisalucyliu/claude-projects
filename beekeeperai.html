context.

A doctor's work stands between life and death. AI-assisted diagnostic tools could make a real difference — but developing reliable clinical algorithms takes time and money that most institutions don't have. In the US, it takes roughly 3 years and costs an average of $5 million to get a single clinical algorithm to generalizability.

The bottleneck isn't compute. It's trust. Algorithm developers need access to varied, high-quality patient data. Data holders need to protect that data from misuse. Both sides own IP that can't be exposed, and neither can afford to compromise.

The problem

A viable algorithm needs to train against varied, high-quality data — but data stewards can't expose that data.

The cost

~3 years and $5M per algorithm. That's the status quo without a secure collaboration platform.

The need

A zero-trust environment where neither side exposes their IP — but can still collaborate.

BeekeeperAI is a zero-trust platform connecting data stewards and algorithm owners so they can collaborate on clinical algorithm development without either party ever exposing their underlying assets.

challenge.

BeekeeperAI was built in two engagements. Version 0 (3 months) was a clickable Figma prototype the client used to demo to investors and generate customer interest — scope focused solely on the matching and contract layer. Its success led directly to Version 1 (6 months), a real MVP product that paying customers could use to run algorithms against datasets and receive validation reports.

Getting requirements from inexperienced product owners

The 4–5 client product owners were experts in medicine, cybersecurity, and hardware — but none had built software before. They had big dreams and less than 5 hours a week to talk to me. How do I get them to agree on a realistic vision and translate that into usable requirements?

No product manager, limited time

V1 was 6 months long, with the first 2–3 months devoted to defining scope and requirements. Without a true product manager, I had to figure out what features to design, prioritize them, and communicate everything to a development team spread across multiple time zones.

V0 — proof of concept.

In V0, I was one of three UX designers working with four client product owners and an engagement manager. Together we uncovered key background concepts in data science and healthcare regulations, established the two main personas, and mapped the initial information architecture.

We met with five data stewards and six algorithm owners for primary research. Contracts emerged as the clearest pain point, so V0's scope focused there.

Key deliverables

As a team of 3 UX designers, we uncovered key background concepts, established the 2 main personas, and mapped the initial IA. During primary research we met with 5 data stewards and 6 algorithm owners and discovered that contracts were a sore point. We gathered requirements through workshops with the 4 product owners.

Final clickable prototype screens

My team and I began with napkin sketches of the final 2–3 features, which translated to low- and then mid-fidelity wireframes. We chose mid-fidelity because the priority of the demos was to show a high-level vision. Key assumptions made that were later disproven in V1 included project organization IA, a single status per project, and the hierarchy of objects.

The client presented this prototype to great success! Investors and future users were so interested that they demanded a real product — which launched V1.

V1 — requirements & research.

In V1, I was the Lead UX Designer managing two other designers, while also filling the product manager role. I worked with the same product owners plus one more, an engagement manager, a scrum master, an architect, and seven developers across Africa and Asia.

During demos, the client product owners found that customers were already contracting and matching outside the system — meaning V0 had missed the actual core value. V1's scope was to build out the core project interaction features so customers could perform their first algorithm runs.

Design roadmap

Our clients lacked a roadmap, and Launch's development side did too. I made a design team timeline based on the length of the project and mapped it onto a calendar in 2-week sprints. The rest of the team followed this calendar as well.

Product questions kickoff

This is the sequence of questions I used to drill down in my design process. Every stage was peeling off layer by layer of information — asking the client product owners questions so they could make decisions, then coaxing more information out of them gradually and systematically.

Workflow mapping and confirmation

The questions didn't get answered in one meeting. I had recurring meetings with all 4–5 client product owners where I continued to coax information that turned into requirements. I zeroed in on the specific parts of each process that were absolutely essential to this MVP.

Site map and feature prioritization

Once I had enough information, I built a sitemap of all proposed features. I noticed how most of the functionality lay in the bottommost layer, with all the pages above just serving as a way to get there. I ran a workshop with the product owners to nail down which bottom features were most important.

Usability testing with low-fidelity wireframes

I ran a usability study with 3 data stewards and 3 algorithm owners clicking through the V0 prototype. Key findings: generally 1 data steward and 1 algorithm owner per contracted project; algorithm-side users may not be very technical (data scientists, not developers); data stewards are very strict about what algorithm owners can see.

V1 — design & execution.

Pick a UI system for high-fidelity wireframing

Due to the time crunch, I used a pre-existing design system. I worked with my designers and the development team to select MUI, which had excellent developer documentation. We changed only a few colors and kept everything else standard — this let development work from lower-fidelity wireframes, saving significant time.

Slice the site map and wireframes up

I developed what I called a "slice" model to link up the sitemap, wireframes, and Jira stories into a shared paradigm. Each feature group is a "slice" (outlined vertically in the sitemap), each wireframe page is an "epic," and each block on a page is a "story." This let the whole team speak the same modular language.

Final sitemap for wireframing

Based on the top prioritized features, I created a final pared-down sitemap of every page I would design.

Final designs

Home page (data steward view)

The data steward views their multiple projects — each project contains a description, a status, and last updates. Note: a data steward can change their home page view to algorithm owner mode (the top header color changes in response).

Project page (algorithm owner view)

The algorithm owner drills into a project to see their current tests, datasets, keys, and documents.

Algorithm upload process pages

Each project requires 1 algorithm, added via a multi-step wizard. An algorithm owner must first obtain a token from BeekeeperAI, then upload and push their algorithm in a docker container. Note: due to time constraints, this flow was implemented from mid-fidelity wireframes.

Initiate a run pages

An algorithm owner can initiate a run after importing all the pieces to create a test, then initiating that test. Note: due to time constraints, this flow was also implemented from mid-fidelity wireframes.

results.

BeekeeperAI successfully launched in April 2022. EscrowAI is listed as a SaaS offering on the Microsoft Azure Marketplace and as a partner solution in Azure confidential computing. The platform continues to grow: recent collaborators include Massive Bio, datosX Digital Health Labs, and cStructure.

Beyond MVP

Post-launch, I gathered all the initiatives discussed but ultimately deprioritized for the V1 MVP effort. These included platform-wide notifications, context-switching between Data Steward and Algorithm Owner views, and the admin experience.

learnings.

This was my first time owning a product from start to finish, and it marked my shift from mid-level designer to lead. I found my niche here: B2B and technical products.

  • If there's no roadmap, build one. Taking initiative gave the whole team momentum to move forward.
  • Play Good Cop and Bad Cop together. With our product owners, I played Good Cop ("what can we do?") while my dev manager partner played Bad Cop ("no, we can't do that"). We triangulated using our complementary perspectives.
  • If you don't understand something, other people probably don't either. It's always worth the extra time to ask questions, even if it means feeling slow in the moment.
  • End-to-end ownership is the kind of work I want to keep doing. I discovered I valued design freedom and full lifecycle ownership — and I haven't stopped looking for it since.
